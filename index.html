Ten Seconds

Stop a hidden clock at exactly 10 seconds. Then try it while your mind is busy.

About 90 seconds

How to play

Six tries. Press start, then press stop when you think 10 seconds have passed. There's no clock to watch.

Quiet tries: just wait. Busy tries: numbers flash up. Tap odd or even for each one, and still stop at 10 seconds.

Please don't count in your head. Counting turns it into a counting test. Go by feel. To stop partway, hold the Quit button for one second, or press Esc twice on a computer.

The science behind this game

What it measures

This game measures how much a busy mind bends your sense of time. You produce a 10-second interval by feel, once with nothing to do and once while doing a simple task. Your score, the stretch, is how much longer your busy intervals ran than your quiet ones.

How this compares to the lab. Researchers call this the production method: people are told a duration and make it themselves. Because you know in advance that you're timing, it's a "prospective" judgment. The lab measures the same direction of effect, but usually reports it as a standardized effect size rather than a percent [1].

What the research says

Busy minds make longer intervals. A review that pooled 117 experiments found that under higher mental load, people judging time as it passes experience durations as shorter. When they produce an interval, as in this game, they make it longer. The production method showed the largest effect of any method [1].

Why: attention is a gate. One leading model says attention to time opens a gate that lets ticks from an internal pacemaker reach a counter. When a task pulls your attention away, fewer ticks get counted, so less time seems to pass [1]. The internal clock idea goes back to a 1963 model with a pacemaker, a counter, and a comparator, which the same review describes [1].

Timing gets less precise as intervals grow. In animal timing, the spread of timing errors grows in proportion to the interval, so the relative error stays about constant [2].

Short productions show it too. When people produced 2- and 5-second intervals while doing a memory task at the same time, their productions became longer and less consistent [3].

Looking back is different. When people judge a duration only afterward, higher load tends to make it seem longer. But that effect wasn't found for durations under 60 seconds [1].

Fear stretches memory, not perception. People who fell 31 meters backward into a safety net later remembered their fall as about 36% longer than others' falls. They couldn't read a fast-flashing number display any better during the fall, so time didn't truly slow down. The stretch was in memory [4].

Adult norms

No published adult norm exists for producing 10 seconds in this way, and the research doesn't report the load effect as a percent. So this game doesn't compare you with a norm. The direction is well established: busy minds produce longer intervals [1].

What's still debated

Researchers don't agree that the brain has a single dedicated clock. One view holds that timing comes from specialized mechanisms. Another says timing arises from the natural dynamics of many brain networks that aren't dedicated to time [5][6]. The attention effect in this game holds up either way.

Limits of this game

One round is noisy. In our simulation of 5,000 rounds by players with a known stretch, one round landed within about ±29 points of the true value 95% of the time. Pooling 10 rounds narrows that to about ±10 points for most players. Very inconsistent timers may need more.

Counting defeats it. If you count in your head, you're measuring counting, not your sense of time.

Small-sample fix. With only three tries of each kind, a plain ratio of averages leans 1 to 2 points high. The game applies a small correction, which removed that lean in the simulation.

The simulation is a model. Its noise levels are our assumptions. Your own consistency, shown once you settle, is the better guide for you.
