Ten Seconds

Stop a hidden clock at exactly 10 seconds. Then try it while your mind is busy.

About 90 seconds

Six tries, about a minute and a half. Press start, then press stop when you think 10 seconds have passed. There's no clock to watch.

Quiet tries: just wait. Busy tries: numbers flash up. Tap odd or even for each one, and still stop at 10 seconds.

Please don't count in your head. Counting turns it into a counting test. Just go by feel. You'll see your times at the end.

Why busy time feels shorter

Your brain has no real clock. It judges time partly by how much attention it pays to time passing. Think of it as a counter that only ticks when you're paying attention to the clock.

Busy attention means fewer ticks. When you're tapping odd or even, most of your attention goes to the numbers. Fewer ticks get counted, so 10 real seconds feels like less. You wait longer before it feels like 10.

A watched pot never boils. The same thing works in reverse. With nothing to do, all your attention goes to time, the counter ticks fast, and waiting feels long.

Looking back is different. This game asks you to judge time as it happens. When people judge time looking back, busy periods can seem longer, because they left more memories. That's why a packed vacation flies by but seems long afterward.

What this can't tell you. Six tries is a small sample, and some people count in their heads even when asked not to. The odd and even numbers appear at uneven times on purpose, so they can't be used as a beat to count along with. The history table across several runs is more reliable than one result.

The science

  • The internal clock idea. An early model proposed a pacemaker in the brain sending out pulses, with a counter adding them up. More pulses counted means more time felt. Treisman, 1963
  • Errors grow with length. People's time judgments get less precise the longer the interval. Being off by 1 second on 10 is about as accurate as being off by 10 seconds on 100. This is called the scalar property. Gibbon, 1977
  • Attention is the gate. The attentional-gate model added a gate between the pacemaker and the counter. Pulses only get through when you pay attention to time. Busy tasks narrow the gate. Zakay and Block, 1997
  • Confirmed across many studies. A review combining results from many experiments found that mental workload makes time judged in the moment seem shorter, and makes time judged in hindsight seem longer. Block, Hancock and Zakay, 2010
  • Fear stretches memory, not perception. People dropped 31 meters into a safety net remembered the fall as lasting longer than it did. But they couldn't read a fast-flashing number during the fall, which they should have if time truly slowed. The stretch was in memory. Stetson, Fiesta and Eagleman, 2007
  • The holiday paradox. A new, busy trip seems to fly by while it's happening, then feels long when you look back. Routine weeks feel the opposite. Writer Claudia Hammond named this the holiday paradox. Hammond, 2012

What's still debated. Researchers don't agree that a single internal clock exists at all. Some think timing comes from the natural rhythms of many brain networks, with no central pacemaker. The attention effect in this game holds up either way. What's debated is the machinery behind it.

Based on Treisman 1963, Zakay and Block 1997, and Block, Hancock and Zakay 2010.
